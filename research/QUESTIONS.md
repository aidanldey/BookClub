# Fan Page Research Question Bank

The master list of questions that drive every book fan page. Each group maps to
one section of the page template, so answers drop straight into the layout.

Questions marked **[core]** must be answered for every book — a page can't ship
without them. Questions marked **[color]** are optional depth: use them when the
research turns up something good, skip them when it doesn't. Don't pad a page
with thin answers to hit a section count.

---

## 1. Identity — "what is this book?"

Section: page header + at-a-glance card.

- **[core]** Title, author, first publication year, original language.
- **[core]** How would a reader who loves this book describe it in one sentence,
  without spoilers?
- **[core]** What genre shelf does it actually sit on, versus the shelf it gets
  filed under? (e.g. *Frankenstein* is shelved horror, read as SF, argued about
  as Romantic tragedy.)
- **[core]** Page count / typical reading time, and whether readers describe it
  as a fast or slow read.
- **[core]** Is it standalone or part of a series? If a series: reading order,
  and is it finished?
- **[color]** Notable translations — which one do readers argue is the best, and
  why? (Critical for Dostoevsky, Dumas, Hugo, Bulgakov, Márquez, Murakami.)
- **[color]** Notable editions readers seek out (illustrated, annotated,
  anniversary, specific cover art).

## 2. Resonance — "why does this book stick with people?"

Section: "Why readers love it" — the emotional heart of the page.

- **[core]** What is the single most common reason readers give for loving this
  book? Look for the reason that repeats across dozens of threads, not the
  cleverest reason one person gave.
- **[core]** What emotion do readers most often report feeling — and *when* in
  the book do they report feeling it?
- **[core]** What do readers say the book did *to* them? (Changed how they saw
  their family, made them cry on a train, made them re-read the last page three
  times, made them call someone.)
- **[core]** What life stage or circumstance makes this book land hardest?
  ("Read it at 19 and it was fine; read it at 35 with a kid and it wrecked me.")
- **[core]** Is this a book readers re-read? What do they say they notice on a
  second pass that they missed the first time?
- **[color]** What do readers say they'd give to read it again for the first
  time — and what would they warn their past self about?
- **[color]** Which specific scene do readers name most often as the moment the
  book "got" them?
- **[color]** What does this book get compared to — "if you loved X, read this"
  and "this is the only book that ever made me feel like X did."

## 3. Craft — "what do readers admire about how it's made?"

Section: "What readers point to."

- **[core]** What craft element do readers praise most: prose, structure,
  characters, worldbuilding, plot, humor, ideas, ending?
- **[core]** What specifically about that element? ("Great prose" is not an
  answer — find the sentence-level thing readers describe.)
- **[core]** What does this book do that readers say no other book does?
- **[color]** What's the most-discussed structural or formal choice? (Death as
  narrator, the footnotes, the four-part Canterbury structure, the missing
  chapter, the second-person turn.)
- **[color]** What's the most quoted opening line or closing line, and what do
  readers say about it?
- **[color]** Does the book have a "difficulty curve" readers warn about — a
  slow first N pages, a hard middle, a section people bounce off?

## 4. Characters — "who do readers love?"

Section: "Reader favorites" — character cards, ideally with a fan-vote widget.

- **[core]** Which 3–6 characters get named most often as favorites? Rank them
  by how often they come up, not by page time.
- **[core]** For each: what's the reason readers give for loving them? Pull the
  specific trait or moment, not a personality summary.
- **[core]** Which character is most *argued about* — loved by half the readers,
  disliked by the other half? Represent both sides fairly.
- **[core]** Which character do readers say broke their heart?
- **[color]** Which minor character has a disproportionate fandom?
- **[color]** Which character do readers say they *became* / saw themselves in?
- **[color]** Best-loved relationship or dynamic (not necessarily romantic).
- **[color]** Which character does the fandom's opinion of shift on re-read?

## 5. Quotes — "what lines do readers carry around?"

Section: "Lines readers keep" — pull-quote blocks.

- **[core]** The 5–10 lines from the book most often quoted by readers, in order
  of how often they appear.
- **[core]** For each line: where it sits in the book (roughly), whether it's a
  spoiler, and what readers say it means to them.
- **[core]** Which line gets tattooed, printed, put in wedding vows, or used as
  a senior quote?
- **[color]** Which line is most often quoted *out of context* or misattributed,
  and what's the actual context?
- **[color]** The line readers say is technically minor but hits hardest.
- **[color]** Is there a passage readers recommend reading aloud?

> **Length limit:** for books still in copyright, quote sparingly — a sentence
> or two, never a full paragraph or poem. Public-domain books (pre-1930 in the
> US, roughly) can take longer excerpts. See `SOURCING.md`.

## 6. Reader voices — "what do actual readers say?"

Section: "From the club" — attributed reader testimonial cards. This is the
section that makes the page feel like a community instead of a wiki.

- **[core]** 6–12 short reader comments (1–3 sentences each) that show the range
  of why people love the book. Each needs a username, source community, date,
  and permalink.
- **[core]** At least one comment that is *critical* or ambivalent. A page where
  everyone agrees reads like marketing.
- **[core]** At least one "I resisted this book and was wrong" comment — these
  convert browsers into readers better than any praise.
- **[core]** At least one comment about the reading *experience* rather than the
  book (where they read it, how fast, what they did after finishing).
- **[color]** A comment that captures the funniest or most specific reaction.
- **[color]** A comment from a re-reader comparing passes.
- **[color]** A comment recommending a specific translation/edition/audiobook.

## 7. Debates — "what does the fandom argue about?"

Section: "Contested" — a discussion prompt block that seeds club conversation.

- **[core]** What's the single most recurring argument about this book?
- **[core]** What's the strongest case each side makes? Steelman both.
- **[core]** Is the book's reputation contested — is there a live "overrated"
  argument, and what's it based on?
- **[color]** Is there a canonical interpretive question with no settled answer?
  (Is the ending real? Is the narrator lying? Is X actually dead?)
- **[color]** What do first-time readers commonly misread?
- **[color]** Does the fandom disagree about the ending?

## 8. Practical — "should I read it, and how?"

Section: "Before you start" — the sidebar.

- **[core]** Who is this book *for*? Who reliably bounces off it?
- **[core]** Content warnings, stated plainly and without judgment.
- **[core]** Audiobook: is there a narrator readers specifically recommend or
  warn against? Is audio the recommended format for this one?
- **[core]** What do readers wish they'd known before starting?
- **[color]** Best companion material — a good intro essay, a map, a family tree,
  a character list readers keep open.
- **[color]** Adaptations: which do readers say is worth it, and should you watch
  before or after?
- **[color]** For series/long books: where's the commitment point — the page at
  which readers say "if it hasn't got you by here, it won't."

## 9. Onward — "what do I read next?"

Section: "If you loved this."

- **[core]** 4–6 books readers most often recommend to people who loved this one,
  each with the one-line reason for the pairing.
- **[core]** The gateway book by the same author, if this one isn't it.
- **[color]** The "if you liked the *idea* but not the execution" alternative.
- **[color]** The non-obvious pairing that regulars recommend and outsiders don't.

---

## Cross-cutting rules

1. **Frequency over eloquence.** The job is to report what many readers say, not
   to find the best-written single comment. Note roughly how common a take is:
   *near-universal*, *common*, *a vocal minority*, *one person said it well*.
2. **Specificity over adjectives.** "Beautiful prose" is not a finding. "Readers
   quote the same three-sentence description of the Salinas Valley" is.
3. **Never invent a reader.** Every reader quote traces to a real, linkable
   comment. See `SOURCING.md`.
4. **Spoilers are tagged, not avoided.** Mark every spoiler answer so the page
   can hide it behind a reveal.
5. **Report absence.** If a question has no real answer for this book, say
   "no consensus found" rather than manufacturing one.
